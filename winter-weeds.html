Weed guide

Winter weeds: Poa annua, henbit, and chickweed

Annual bluegrass, henbit, and chickweed germinate in the fall, sit through winter, and bloom when you are ready to think about spring. The control that works is aimed at that life cycle, and it is different on dormant Bermuda than it is on tall fescue.

Three weeds, one season

Poa annua, annual bluegrass, is a grass. It germinates as soil cools in late summer and fall, lives as a low clump through winter, seeds heavily in spring, and dies when the heat arrives. In a Bermuda lawn it is the bright green tuft in an otherwise tan yard, and the seed it drops is next year’s problem. In a Kentucky bluegrass or tall fescue lawn it hides better, because it is a grass inside a grass, and it still dies out in June and leaves a hole.

Henbit and common chickweed are winter annual broadleaves. They germinate in the same cool, moist fall window, stay small through the cold, and flower late winter into early spring. Henbit has square stems and purple flowers. Chickweed makes a mat of small rounded leaves. Deadnettle is often in the same patch as henbit. The control approach is the same even when the flower is different: get them while they are small and growing, or stop the germination in the first place.

They are not a sign that the lawn “needs fertilizer.” Nitrogen on a lawn full of chickweed feeds the chickweed. Identify the weed, then pick a tool that matches the grass you are trying to keep.

Fall pre-emergent, and the seeding conflict

Virginia Tech’s warm-season calendar separates the two pre-emergent seasons. Spring pre-emergent is for crabgrass and the other summer annual grasses. Fall pre-emergent is for annual bluegrass and the winter broadleaves, including henbit and chickweed. The fall product goes down before those seeds germinate, which means late summer into early fall, while the soil is cooling, not in January when the weeds are already up and blooming.

A pre-emergent does not care that you like the grass you are about to seed. The same herbicide that stops Poa annua stops perennial ryegrass and tall fescue. If you are overseeding Bermuda with rye, or thickening a fescue lawn, you skip the fall pre-emergent. Virginia Tech says this outright: consider what the pre-emergent will do to any desirable seed before you spray it. You can have winter color, or you can have fall Poa prevention. A standard pre-emergent will not give you both.

If you are not seeding, the fall pre-emergent is the cleaner tool, especially for Poa annua, which is harder to kill selectively once it is up inside a cool-season lawn. Water it in if the label says to. A product sitting on dry thatch is not a herbicide yet. Write down the date. Many labels expect a second application later in the fall for season-long Poa control. The interval is on that label, not in a national calendar.

Dormant Bermuda is a special case

When Bermuda is fully tan and dormant, and you did not overseed it, Virginia Tech says there is an opening for non-selective post-emergent herbicides on the winter weeds. The Bermuda is not growing. The Poa, henbit, and chickweed are. A non-selective product can be used in that window because the desirable grass is dormant, not because the herbicide has become polite.

That window is narrower than the ads suggest. The Bermuda has to be fully dormant, not “mostly brown with green stems in the low spots.” A green Bermuda sprig will die. Seeded Bermuda and some cold-tender varieties are less forgiving than a thick hybrid that has been straw-colored for weeks. Do not do this on tall fescue, bluegrass, ryegrass, or a mixed lawn. Those grasses are your lawn, and a non-selective herbicide will kill them in any month. Do not do it on overseeded rye you intend to keep. And do not do it in spring as the Bermuda is greening up. Virginia Tech also says not to apply post-emergent broadleaf herbicides during the spring transition.

Weeds still have to be growing for a post-emergent to work. Virginia Tech’s threshold for cool-season weeds is roughly air temperatures of 50°F or warmer. A herbicide on henbit that is shut down by a hard freeze mostly becomes an expensive stain. Read the label for the temperature window, the rate, and whether the product is even labeled on dormant Bermuda. This page will not invent a glyphosate rate. The label is the rate.

Cool-season lawns do not get the dormant trick

Tall fescue and bluegrass do not offer a safe month for a non-selective herbicide. You are left with three honest tools. One is a fall pre-emergent timed before germination, if you are not seeding. One is a selective post-emergent: a broadleaf herbicide for henbit and chickweed, applied when the weeds are small, the grass is not stressed, and the label’s temperature range is met. Grass weeds like Poa annua need a different active ingredient than the broadleaf product, and many of those products are picky about species, seeding, and temperature. The third tool is time and mowing. A thick fall stand, mowed at about 3 inches, shades out a share of the seedlings that a thin, scalped lawn invites.

Spring, when henbit is purple and pretty, is the worst time to start. The weed has already made most of its growth, and flowering plants are harder to kill and about to drop seed. Fall and early winter, on a mild day the label allows, is the post-emergent window. Spot-spray. Do not blanket a lawn with weed-and-feed because you saw three henbit plants. Weed-and-feed is also a fertilizer, and late nitrogen on the wrong grass, or nitrogen inside a state blackout, is its own mistake. See winterizer timing before you buy a combination bag.

What you should see if it worked

A fall pre-emergent that was watered in shows up as bare soil instead of a carpet of chickweed, not as dead weeds curling overnight. Pre-emergent does not kill what is already up. A post-emergent on henbit or chickweed should twist and stall those plants over one to two weeks if the weather stayed inside the label. Poa annua that was already tillering may only be suppressed. If the green tufts are still seeding in April, plan the fall pre-emergent earlier next year and keep the mower up.

Walk the lawn once in late winter and write down where the weeds were. The same thin, compacted, or overwatered spots repeat. Aeration, the right mowing height, and not overseeding into a pre-emergent are the cultural half of this. The spring pre-emergent guide is the other half of the year, aimed at crabgrass rather than these winter annuals. They are not the same application.

Questions people ask

When do Poa annua, henbit, and chickweed germinate?

In the fall, as soils cool and stay moist. They grow through winter and flower in late winter or early spring. A herbicide applied after they have flowered is late.

Will a spring crabgrass preventer stop them?

No. Spring pre-emergent is aimed at summer annuals like crabgrass. Winter annuals need a fall application, before they germinate, or a post-emergent after they are up.

Can I spray glyphosate on winter weeds in Bermuda?

Only if the Bermuda is fully dormant, you did not overseed it, and the product label allows that use. Green Bermuda, tall fescue, bluegrass, and ryegrass are not dormant in a way that survives a non-selective herbicide.

Can I prevent Poa annua and still overseed with ryegrass?

Not with a standard fall pre-emergent. The herbicide that stops annual bluegrass will stop the ryegrass too. Choose winter color or choose the pre-emergent.

When should I spray henbit?

While it is small and actively growing, on a day that meets the label’s temperature window, often anytime mild weather holds in fall or winter. Waiting until it blooms in spring means more seed and a harder kill.
