Timing guide

Spring pre-emergent timing by region

Crabgrass germinates when the soil warms, not when a national calendar says March. The South starts watching first. The North starts watching last. The application itself waits for your soil temperature and the label.

The decision is a soil temperature, not a state

A pre-emergent herbicide stops seedlings as they try to come through the treated layer of soil. It does not kill crabgrass you can already see. So the application has to land before germination and then be watered in, if that is what the label requires, so the product moves off the leaf and into the top of the soil.

This site’s soil-temperature tool treats about 2 inches, the 6-centimeter reading, as the depth that matters, and it flags 50–55°F held for several days as the crabgrass window, following University of Minnesota and Penn State guidance published on that page. A Purdue crabgrass note linked from when to apply pre-emergent describes germination nearer 60°F. Use both as a reason to be early. If you wait until you are sure the soil has been warm for a week, a share of the seed has already started. Apply when the 2-inch temperature is approaching that range, on a day you can water the product in, and before you see crabgrass seedlings.

Forsythia bloom and “when the redbuds open” are rough local rhymes for the same warming. They are not a substitute for a thermometer if you have one. Open the soil temperature tracker, pick the grass, and read the 2-inch number for your ZIP. Air temperature on the noon news runs ahead of the soil and will talk you into spraying too soon.

South: start watching in late winter

Along the Gulf Coast, across Texas and Oklahoma, and through the coastal plain of Georgia and the Carolinas, the soil at 2 inches reaches the germination range while northern lawns are still frozen. Late winter into early spring is when you look, not when a single date forces the spray. A warm February can open the window. A cold one pushes it. Florida is its own case: South Florida turf grows through much of the year, and a Midwest “spray in April” habit does not describe it. Use local extension timing there instead of importing a Kansas date.

The grass under that pre-emergent is usually Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, or centipede. The pre-emergent is for the crabgrass, not a signal to fertilize. Virginia Tech says warm-season nitrogen waits until spring green-up is actually finished. People stack a pre-emergent with a heavy nitrogen “wake up” feeding and then wonder why the Bermuda was soft and disease-prone. Split those decisions. The herbicide can go down while the lawn is still brown. The fertilizer waits for growth.

St. Augustine and centipede are sensitive to some active ingredients that Bermuda tolerates. The label’s turf list is not fine print. If your lawn is St. Augustine, confirm the product before you buy the one your neighbor uses on Bermuda.

Transition zone: March and April are the watch months

From Virginia and Maryland through the North Carolina piedmont, Tennessee, Kentucky, Missouri, and Kansas, the soil usually enters the crabgrass range after the lower South and before the Great Lakes. March and April are the months to be reading the thermometer, not a promise that every lawn is ready on March 15. A south-facing slope in Nashville and a shaded tall fescue lawn in the same city are not on the same day.

This is also where two lawns share a yard. A pre-emergent that is legal on the Bermuda in the front may be the wrong month, or the wrong chemical, for the tall fescue in the back if you seeded that fescue in the fall and the reseeding interval is still running. Read the interval. Prodiamine in particular can block seed for a long time. If the fescue is thin and you need to seed, the overseeding guide explains why fall was the time, and why a spring pre-emergent and spring seed fight each other.

Maryland’s fertilizer blackout, which runs past November 15 until March 1 for homeowners, is about nitrogen and phosphorus. It is not a pre-emergent ban. Do not confuse the two, and do not use a weed-and-feed to “solve” both jobs with one pass if the fertilizer half is still illegal or the herbicide half is the wrong week. The fertilizer rules are on the winterizer page.

North: the window is later, and it is still a soil temperature

In the northern Midwest, the Great Lakes, and New England, crabgrass pre-emergent is often an April or early May conversation. The cue is still the soil, not Tax Day. A warm spring moves it forward. Snow on the ground in the first week of April means you are watching, not spraying. University of Minnesota’s lawn calendar is the regional backbone for these cool-season lawns, and it still expects you to adjust for the year you are in.

The grass is tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, ryegrass, or a mix. It may already be growing, and greening up, before crabgrass germinates. That green-up tempts a heavy spring nitrogen application in the same weekend as the pre-emergent. Purdue AY-22 wants spring nitrogen modest and slow-release, because the flush is coming anyway. Put the herbicide down on time. Keep the fertilizer light, or save it for fall. The cool-season guide is the rest of that program.

Split applications, goosegrass, and the second date

Crabgrass is the first summer annual. Goosegrass germinates later, often in warmer soil, which is why a single early application sometimes looks like it “failed” in June when the plant coming up is not crabgrass. Some labels allow a split: part of the rate early, the rest several weeks later, which stretches the barrier. The gap is printed on the product. It is often on the order of weeks, not a second guess you invent in July. If the label does not offer a split, do not apply the full rate twice and call it insurance. You will blow past the annual maximum.

Dithiopyr has some early post-emergent effect on very young crabgrass. Prodiamine does not. Pendimethalin is its own label. The comparison that matters before you buy is on prodiamine versus dithiopyr, and the price of the actual bag is on the price board. None of that replaces the rate table for your square footage.

Rain or irrigation after the application is part of the application. If the label says water it in within a few days, a dry week leaves the product on the foliage and in the thatch, where it does less and where the mower can remove it. Log the day you watered it in. That is the day the clock starts, not the day the bag was delivered.

What to record so next year is easier

Write down the product, the active ingredient, the pounds or ounces per 1,000 square feet, the soil temperature that morning, and whether you seeded last fall. Next winter you will not remember which side of the house was early. The regional months above only tell you when to start checking. The 2-inch reading tells you when to load the spreader.

Questions people ask

What soil temperature means it is time for crabgrass pre-emergent?

Apply before crabgrass germinates. This site’s soil tracker uses 50–55°F at about 2 inches for several days, based on Minnesota and Penn State. Purdue has described germination nearer 60°F. Treat that range as the window you want to be ahead of, and water the product in if the label says to.

When does the South usually hit that temperature?

Late winter into early spring across Texas, Oklahoma, the Gulf Coast, and the coastal Southeast. The exact week moves with the year. South Florida does not follow a Midwest spring calendar.

When do the North and the transition zone hit it?

The transition zone is often watching in March and April. The northern Midwest and Northeast are often watching in April and early May. Soil temperature at your ZIP overrules both.

Can I apply pre-emergent and seed in the spring?

Not with most products. They stop desirable grass seed too. Siduron is the historical exception for some cool-season seedings, and it is not a season-long crabgrass barrier. Fall seeding avoids the conflict.

Do I fertilize on the same day?

You do not have to. Warm-season lawns should wait on nitrogen until they have actually greened up. Cool-season lawns only want a light spring feeding, if any. A weed-and-feed locks you into both decisions at once.
