Fall lawn care schedule

Fall is the main working season for tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, and ryegrass. It is the season to stop pushing Bermuda, zoysia, and St. Augustine, and to get the lawn ready for cold.

Two different falls

A national “feed and seed” ad is written for one kind of lawn. Cool-season grasses — tall fescue, fine fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, and perennial ryegrass — grow hardest when days shorten and soil cools. Warm-season grasses — Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, and centipede — are doing the opposite. They are storing what they can and heading toward dormancy.

Use this page as the order of operations, then adjust it with the soil temperature tracker, a soil test, and the label on the product in your hand. The cool-season guide and the Bermuda guide cover the rest of the year. The late-fall nitrogen decision has its own page: when to apply winterizer fertilizer.

Cool-season lawns: this is the season

Purdue AY-22, the extension publication on fertilizing established cool-season lawns, says these grasses benefit most when most of the nitrogen goes down from late summer through autumn. That feeding recovers turf worn down by summer, thickens the stand, and sets the plant up for winter without a surge of soft growth.

AY-22 splits fall into two jobs. From late August through early November, the publication says you can apply up to 1 pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet each month when you want density. From early October until early December, it switches the suggestion to mostly water-soluble nitrogen at a lighter rate, 0.5 to 0.75 pound per 1,000 square feet, about every three weeks. Those are program ranges for an established lawn, not a reason to empty a bag. Read the fertilizer math on the fertilizer calculator and stay inside the annual total that fits how you maintain the lawn.

Purdue is also plain about when to stop. Apply fertilizer only to turf that is still growing. Do not fertilize dormant or badly drought-stressed grass, and do not fertilize in winter or when the soil is frozen. Sweep granules off sidewalks and driveways back into the turf so the next rain does not carry them into the street.

Some states close the window earlier than the agronomy does. Maryland is the example to remember: homeowners may not apply fertilizer that contains nitrogen or phosphorus after November 15 or before March 1. The details, and the warning not to copy Maryland’s dates into another state, are on the winterizer page.

Seed and repair while the soil is kind

Late summer into early fall is the reliable window for seeding tall fescue, bluegrass, and ryegrass. Air temperatures are easing, the soil is still warm enough for roots, and summer annual weeds are fading. University of Minnesota Extension treats fall as the period when shoots grow and the plant stores nutrients in the roots for winter. That is the same reason a thin lawn answers seeding better now than it will in July, or in a rushed April.

Seed has to touch soil. Mow at the normal height, rake out the thatch mat where seed would sit on top, and core-aerate if the lawn is compacted and you can water afterward. Spread seed at a rate meant for overseeding, not a brand-new lawn, unless you are renovating down to bare ground. This site’s seed calculator uses NC State’s 6 pounds per 1,000 square feet as the tall fescue establishment rate and a lighter overseed rate on top of existing turf. Check the seed tag for pure live seed and the variety. A cheap bag that is mostly coating and weed seed is not a bargain.

Keep the surface damp until the seedlings are rooted. That usually means light water more than once a day at first, then less often and deeper. Skip a pre-emergent that will also stop your grass seed. The overseeding guide covers cool-season renovation and the separate job of putting perennial ryegrass onto dormant Bermuda.

Northern extensions, including Minnesota, also describe a late-fall dormant seeding: seed put down when it is too cold to germinate, left to sit until spring. That is a backup for people who missed early fall, not a better plan. Early fall is still the establishment window to aim for.

Mow and deal with leaves

Keep mowing cool-season grass at the regular height until growth actually stops. University of Minnesota late-fall guidance says not to drop the mower a lot at once, and not to go below 2 inches on that last stretch. A single scalp “for winter” exposes crowns, dries the soil, and invites winter annual weeds.

Leaves are the other weekly job. A light layer can be mulched into the canopy and it disappears. A wet mat that sits for days shades the grass, holds moisture on the crowns, and is a common reason a lawn comes out of winter thin. Mulch what the mower can digest. Rake or blow the rest off. Do not pile leaves on the turf and call it compost.

Warm-season lawns: put the spreader away

Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, and centipede do not want a cool-season feeding schedule. Virginia Tech’s warm-season maintenance calendar says to start nitrogen only after spring green-up is complete, and it treats extra nitrogen timing during the cool months as something for overseeded ryegrass, not for the Bermuda underneath.

The same Virginia Tech note caps readily available nitrogen around 0.7 pound per 1,000 square feet per active growing month on warm-season grasses and on ryegrass overseedings, with a higher allowance only for slowly available sources. Annual totals differ by species: Bermuda and St. Augustine can be pushed harder than zoysia or centipede, which the calendar holds to roughly 1 to 2 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet for the year. A “winterizer” dumped on green Bermuda in October is how people buy spring dead spot and a slow green-up.

What fall is for, on warm-season turf, is cleanup and prevention. If the lawn has a history of spring dead spot on Bermuda or large patch on zoysia, Virginia Tech says those diseases infect in early to mid-fall even though you will not see the patches until green-up. That is a fungicide decision based on history and the label, not a fertilizer decision. Raise the mower as growth slows, keep leaves off the dormant canopy, and water only if a warm, dry spell is actually stressing turf that is still green. Once it is brown and dormant, it is not asking for a weekly inch.

Weeds, water, and the record

Fall is also when winter annuals germinate: annual bluegrass (Poa annua), henbit, and common chickweed. A fall pre-emergent aimed at those weeds will also stop ryegrass and any cool-season seed you were about to spread. If you are overseeding, do not put that herbicide down. If you are not seeding, the winter weeds guide walks through the choice between a fall pre-emergent and a later post-emergent on fully dormant Bermuda.

Blow out or drain the sprinkler system before the first hard freeze if you live where pipes freeze. The steps, and the parts a homeowner should not hit with an air compressor, are in how to winterize a sprinkler system.

Before the holidays, pull a soil test if you have not done one in the last few years. Phosphorus and potassium belong on the lawn when the test says so, not because a bag is labeled winterizer. Lime, if a test calls for it, is a fall job on many cool-season lawns because it has all winter to move. Log the test, the seed, and every application. Next October you will want the date, the product, and whether it worked.

Questions people ask

Is fall the best time to fertilize a lawn?

For tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, and ryegrass, yes. Purdue AY-22 puts most of the nitrogen from late summer through autumn, with lighter water-soluble rates from early October into early December while the grass is still growing. Bermuda, zoysia, and St. Augustine should not get that cool-season program.

Should I fertilize Bermuda in the fall?

Do not push nitrogen as Bermuda is going dormant. Virginia Tech starts warm-season nitrogen after spring green-up is finished. Extra nitrogen in the cool months is for an overseeded ryegrass, not for the Bermuda itself.

When should I stop mowing in the fall?

Mow cool-season grass at the normal height until it stops growing. University of Minnesota late-fall guidance says not to lower the height sharply, and not below 2 inches. Warm-season lawns simply stop needing a mow once they go dormant.

Can I seed and apply pre-emergent in the same month?

Not if the pre-emergent also stops grass seed. A fall herbicide aimed at Poa annua will stop the tall fescue or ryegrass you just spread. Seed first and skip that herbicide, or skip seeding and use the pre-emergent. Read the label either way.

Does this schedule apply in Maryland?

The agronomy is the same, but Maryland law is stricter. Homeowners may not apply nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizer after November 15 or before March 1. Check your own state before you copy a date from somewhere else.
