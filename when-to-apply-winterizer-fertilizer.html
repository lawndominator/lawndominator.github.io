Fertilizer guide

When to apply winterizer fertilizer

Winterizer is a store word. The agronomy is a last nitrogen application on a cool-season lawn that is still green, before the ground freezes, at a modest rate. It is not for dormant Bermuda, and in some states it is not legal in late November.

What the bag is selling

A winterizer fertilizer is usually a high-nitrogen product, sometimes with potassium equal to the nitrogen, and a label that says to apply it in October, November, or December. Purdue’s turf note on these products makes the useful distinction. Emphasizing fall fertility is right. Matching potassium to nitrogen on every lawn is not, because potassium only belongs there when a soil test says the soil is short. Widespread potassium “just in case” is how the marketing outruns the soil.

The nitrogen is the part that matters, and only for cool-season grasses that are still growing: tall fescue, fine fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, and perennial ryegrass. The plant takes up that nitrogen while the soil is cool, stores it, and uses it for root reserves and a calmer spring. You are not trying to make the lawn jump in December.

Warm-season lawns are the wrong audience. Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine, and centipede should already be done with nitrogen for the year, unless you overseeded Bermuda with ryegrass and you are feeding the rye, not the Bermuda. That exception is on the overseeding guide, and it is a light in-season rate for the rye, not a winterizer bag on straw-colored turf.

The rate and the calendar

Purdue AY-22 is the publication to follow. From early October until early December it calls for mostly water-soluble nitrogen at 0.5 to 0.75 pound of actual nitrogen per 1,000 square feet, about every three weeks, as the lighter end of a fall program that started with heavier feeding in late August and September. Water-soluble means urea or another quick source the plant can take up before soils get too cold, not a thick coat of slow-release that is still sitting there in January.

Older advice was heavier. A Purdue turf article on whether it is too late to fertilize says the program used to be 1.0 to 1.5 pounds of quick nitrogen per 1,000 square feet in early to mid-November, and that the recommendation was revised down to 0.5 to 0.75 pound. A separate Purdue note on winterizer bags has, in the past, described a last application near 1.0 to 1.25 pounds. When the numbers disagree, use the current AY-22 range and the revised lighter late-fall rate. More nitrogen in cold soil is not stored more efficiently. A share of it stays in the soil and can leach.

Timing inside that window moves with latitude. The same Purdue piece points out that southern Indiana runs about three weeks behind northern Indiana. A mid-November application in the north can look like late November or early December in the south of the same state. The test is not the printed month. The lawn is still green, you are at or just after the last mowing, the soil is not frozen, and snow is not on the ground. If nights are already near freezing and the lawn has stopped growing, you missed it. Purdue’s answer in that situation is to wait, then if the lawn looks thin next spring add a small amount, on the order of 0.25 to 0.5 pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet, after the spring flush, not a makeup dump in January.

Convert the bag with the fertilizer calculator. To apply 0.5 pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet from a 30-0-4, you need about 1.7 pounds of product per 1,000 square feet. The winterizer label’s “this bag covers 5,000 square feet” may not match that rate. Do the math for your measured lawn, then set the spreader to the product, not to a memory.

Soil tests, phosphorus, and potassium

Nitrogen is the nutrient you schedule. Phosphorus and potassium are the nutrients you test. AY-22 says phosphorus and potassium needs should come from a soil test. A winterizer with a big middle number is a phosphorus application, and a lot of states restrict phosphorus on established lawns unless a test shows a need or you are establishing seed. Potassium helps stress tolerance when the soil is actually low. It does not need to mirror the nitrogen number on the bag.

If you have not tested in three or four years, fall is a good time to pull cores before you buy the product. Lime, if the test calls for it, can go down in fall on cool-season lawns and work over winter. Do not lime because the bag was next to the winterizer.

Blackout rules: Maryland, and why you look up your own state

Agronomy and the law are not the same calendar. Maryland is the clear example. For a non-professional, the Code of Maryland Regulations (15.20.10.16) says you may not apply fertilizer containing nitrogen or phosphorus to turf before March 1 or after November 15, or any time the ground is frozen. The Maryland Department of Agriculture describes the homeowner blackout as November 16 through March 1. That is the same rule said from both ends: after November 15 you are in it, and you are not out until March 1.

There is a narrow professional exception, not a homeowner one. Trained applicators may put down up to 0.5 pound of water-soluble nitrogen per 1,000 square feet from November 16 through December 1. Enhanced-efficiency fertilizers are still barred in that blackout. Homeowners do not get the November 16–December 1 opening. University of Maryland Extension states the same prohibition and still agrees, as agronomy, that cool-season grasses would like a chunk of their nitrogen in fall. The law wins.

Maryland also caps a single homeowner application at 0.9 pound of total nitrogen per 1,000 square feet and 0.7 pound of water-soluble nitrogen, and it requires at least 20 percent slow-release nitrogen in ordinary products. So a Purdue-style quick late-fall shot can be both the wrong date and the wrong analysis in Maryland. If you live there, finish nitrogen by November 15, use a legal product, and do not “sneak” a winterizer on the 20th.

Other states, counties, and cities restrict fertilizer dates, phosphorus, or setbacks from water. New rules show up, and the dates are not Maryland’s dates. This guide will not invent a blackout for a state it has not cited. Before you apply, check your state department of agriculture or cooperative extension. If the ground is frozen, or heavy rain is forecast, do not spread fertilizer even if the calendar says you may. Sweep granules off pavement.

Where this sits in the fall schedule

The winterizer, if you are going to use one, is the last act of the fall lawn care schedule, after the September recovery feeding, after seeding has had time to root, and after leaves are under control. It is not a substitute for the earlier fall nitrogen that did the thickening. Purdue is explicit that if you hit August, September, or October, missing the final November shot is not a crisis. If you skipped fall entirely, a cold-soil winterizer will not rewind the year.

Log the analysis, the pounds of product, the square feet, and whether you watered it in lightly. Next fall you will know whether that lawn needed the last shot or whether the September application was the one that mattered.

Questions people ask

What is a winterizer fertilizer?

It is a marketing name for a fall or late-fall lawn fertilizer, usually high in nitrogen and often high in potassium. The useful part for a cool-season lawn is a modest quick-release nitrogen application while the grass is still green. Extra potassium is for a soil test, not for the picture on the bag.

When should I apply it?

On tall fescue, bluegrass, and ryegrass, Purdue AY-22 places water-soluble nitrogen at 0.5 to 0.75 pound per 1,000 square feet from early October into early December, about every three weeks, while the turf is growing and the soil is not frozen. Farther north, that last shot lands earlier than it does in the southern part of the same state.

Can I winterize Bermuda or zoysia?

No. Do not put late nitrogen on warm-season grass that is going dormant. The exception is a light feeding of ryegrass that was overseeded onto Bermuda, and that is not a winterizer on the Bermuda itself.

What is Maryland’s fertilizer blackout?

Homeowners may not apply nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizer after November 15 or before March 1, or when the ground is frozen. Professionals have a short, limited exception from November 16 through December 1 that homeowners do not get.

Is it too late if I missed November?

If the lawn has stopped growing or the soil is frozen, yes. Wait until spring and, if it looks thin, use a small application after the spring flush. Do not spread fertilizer on snow or frozen ground.
